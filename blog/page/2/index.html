How pagination works on this site:

  • /blog/ — newest 24 modern posts.
  • /blog/page/2/ — older window of modern posts.
  • /blog/categories/ — the 24 pre-2026 historical posts, indexed separately and clearly labelled.
  • Canonical of /blog/page/N/ points to itself; rel="prev" / rel="next" link the chain.

The card list below is regenerated by tools/regen-blog-pool.py from assets/data/blogs.json.

Why is the blog split across pages at all?

Because a single list of every post stops being navigable long before it stops being technically possible. Page 1 holds the newest 24 modern posts, which is roughly what a reader will scan before deciding what to open. Everything older moves here rather than extending page 1 indefinitely.

The boundary is a moving one. As new posts publish, the oldest entries on page 1 cross onto this page, so a post's location changes over time even though its own URL never does. If you want to link to an article, link to the article: paginated URLs describe a window, not a document.

Is older writing still maintained?

The distinction is editorial, not chronological. Both this page and the topic index hold older material, but they hold it for different reasons.

Every post on the blog sits on one list, newest first. Older pieces keep their original publication date and, where the field has moved on, carry a note pointing at current coverage of the same topic.

Pages 1 to 3 are one continuous list. For our present position on a subject, start with the newest post in that category; older pieces remain online because the background material is still sound.

Where should you go next?

If you are browsing rather than hunting for one article, topic order usually beats date order. The category pages group every post by subject and list every post on a subject together, so you get the full body of writing on FIDO or transit in one place. Tags cut across categories for subjects that span several. For a specific term, standard or part number, search covers the whole corpus.
